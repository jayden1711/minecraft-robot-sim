## C1. "It's just a game."

Answer: the game is the editor and the display. The learning problem is a standard MuJoCo robot-learning problem; the game only supplies geometry
and task markers, and the viewer. Nothing about the learning depends on Minecraft being present.

Test (M3): train and evaluate with a scene exported to MJCF; delete the Minecraft server and mod from the machine's path; run `rollout.py` from the exported bundle
on 20 held-out seeds. Pass = it runs and reports success rate with the same seeds as the in-game run. M0 half: `tests/test_critiques.py::test_C2_*`
already runs the exported scene with only `mujoco` and `numpy` importable (`python -I`).

## C2. "Minecraft physics is fake."

Answer: it is not used. Minecraft's physics (gravity, collision, entities) never touches a robot. MuJoCo simulates everything; Minecraft only provides block positions.

Test (runs now): export the demo maze through the server code path to an MJCF file; run a seeded rollout in a separate isolated Python process
(`python -I`, no `mcrobot` importable, no sockets) twice: the trajectory hashes must be bit-identical, differ for a different seed (non-vacuous), and equal the in-process rollout.
`server/tests/test_critiques.py::test_C2_*`. Repeat at M3 on the trained-policy rollout with the Minecraft server process not running (check with `pgrep`, recorded in the run log).

## C3. "Blocks aren't real terrain."

Answer: blocks are a coarse, editable, shareable terrain format; they are exact for what they claim (axis-aligned boxes at known scale), and the robot only sees
MuJoCo geometry. They are also not the end state: M4 rebuilds a real room to scale and runs on a real rover, which is the test of whether block-built worlds transfer.

Tests:
1. (runs now) Geometry fidelity: the exported collision boxes cover exactly the solid cells (disjoint, exact), partial blocks keep their real sub-block boxes, and the coordinate mapping is a rotation with a round trip. `test_boxes.py`, `test_scene.py`.
2. (M2) Seam sensitivity: train/evaluate Go2 on the same terrain with merged boxes vs one box per block; report the gap in success and in contact-force spikes. If seams hurt, merge differently or fillet edges.
3. (M2) Scale: same world at two `meters_per_block` values; policy success vs step height is reported, not assumed.
4. (M4) Real-vs-sim: the room rebuilt from measurements, rover run on the real floor; success sim vs real, with the gap stated.

## C4. "Toy tasks."

Answer: M1 is a toy by design (reach a goal in a maze) because it proves the pipeline. The point is the pipeline and what it allows next. We do not claim more than we measure.

Status after M1: the owner changed M1 from a rover in a maze to the G1 humanoid in the sky arena, and the evaluation became one fixed course (not 50 mazes): success rate, time to finish and falls over several evaluation seeds from the command block, plus a scripted compass-following baseline (no learning) for scale. One course is a weaker test than 50 held-out mazes; the maze-family evaluation below is still the M1 target for generalisation and is not done. Two things make this task easier than it looks and are stated plainly: the policy walks on a frozen locomotion policy (it only chooses velocity commands), and it is given a compass (the direction to the next point on the shortest walkable path, computed from the exported map) besides its ray sensing, so it is not a mapless explorer.
Tests: each milestone has a pre-stated held-out evaluation, defined before training:
- M1: 50 mazes the policy never saw (player-built plus randomly generated by the same exporter), success rate over seeds with confidence intervals; a straight-line-to-goal baseline and a random policy for scale.
- M2: terrain families with increasing step height; report where Go2 and G1 stop succeeding, including the failures.
- M4: a task on the real rover in the real room.
"Toy" is then a statement about difficulty with numbers behind it, not a label.

## C5. "It's just a scripted animation."

Answer: every ghost pose shown in game is a logged env state from the training run (PLAN.md, Ghosts), and the final policy replays in plain MuJoCo on held-out seeds.

Tests (M1):
1. Provenance: `ghost_log.npz` is written by the sender (every sample sent, with step and env id). For a sampled (step, env) the check re-derives the pose from the training checkpoint at that step with the same seed and compares to the logged pose within tolerance; a scripted animation cannot do this.
2. Learning is real: ghost success fraction from the log rises over training and is not time-based; a run with the reward zeroed (control) must not rise.
3. Held-out replay: load `policy` + `scene.xml` in plain MuJoCo (no mcrobot, no Minecraft), run N seeds never used in training, success rate with seeds recorded; per-seed trajectories must reproduce bit-identically across two runs.
4. Frame check: in-game screenshot of ghosts at step t and the MuJoCo render of the logged states at step t show the same positions in the same world.
Status after M1 (navigation on the sky arena, `server/tests/test_c5_provenance.py`): test 1 runs in a reduced form. The ghost streamer logs every pose it sends (env step, ghost, pelvis position); the test re-runs the same seeds and policy with nothing streamed and compares the logged poses with the re-derived ones (a few millimetres, the logged value is the last physics substep), and a control with a different env seed must NOT match. What it does not do yet: re-derive from a training checkpoint at an arbitrary past step (checkpoints keep only latest, best and final by the NUC rule), the zero-reward control run (test 2), the plain-MuJoCo held-out replay (test 3, M3) and the screenshot comparison (test 4). `test_C5_ghosts_are_logged_training_states` in `test_critiques.py` stays skipped as the placeholder for tests 2 to 4.
Honest caveat for the swarm: the 20 ghosts are the first 20 of the parallel envs, which always start on the command block (the other envs start anywhere on the course), so the swarm shows the evaluation condition, not a random sample of all episodes; the success rate reported in the run log counts all envs and, separately, the spawn-start ones.

## C6. "The ghost you watch might not be what the simulator is doing" (display failures, stale ghosts)

Answer: a ghost that is invisible, frozen or left over from an earlier run is a display fault, and the mod now makes it loud instead of silent.

What went wrong once: a first mod build wrote `block_state` in a form that 26.3 decodes to air, the invisible entities were saved in the world, and a later build reused them by UUID, so the demo ran but showed nothing. Controls now in place (mod, tested in `GhostCommandsTest`, `BridgeReconnectTest` and the smoke test `scripts/smoke_server.sh`):
1. Every streaming session gets its own tag and UUIDs; the first spawn removes all existing ghost entities; a ghost entity that loads outside a session (saved in an unloaded chunk) is discarded on load; stop, trainer loss and server shutdown remove them, so none stay in the world save.
2. After summoning, the mod reads every part back from the server and reports an ERROR in the log and in chat if a part is missing or reads back as air.
3. Spawn, stop (with the reason), and a 30-second heartbeat (poses received, age of the last pose) are logged, so a frozen or stalled stream is visible.
4. The bridge reconnects by itself when the trainer restarts and says only "connected" or "disconnected".
Limit: the read-back proves the entities exist with a non-air block; that the poses match the simulator is still the C5 provenance test.

## C7. "Your reward could not even be earned" (a goal rule written for a point robot)

What went wrong once: the goal and plate rules counted a robot as "on" a block when its tracked point was within 0.5 m above the block top. A standing G1's pelvis rides 0.78 m above the floor, so the rule never fired for it, and a policy trained on it would have been optimising a goal it could never reach. Control now in place: the rules take the robot's base height (`tasks.G1_BASE_HEIGHT`), and `test_goal_rule.py` checks, on the arena's real gold pad, that the old rule fails for a standing G1, the new one fires, and that a point that is too high, too far or fallen far below does not. The batched training version (`navtask.TorchTask`) uses the same rule, and `test_navenv.py` checks that standing on the pad ends the episode with the goal reward.

## C8. "The hard parts of the course were left out of training" (ice, pushes)

Answer: arena v2 adds low-friction ice and three scheduled pushes on the pelvis, and the training environment has both; they are not only in the demo.
Controls: the MJCF loader keeps the ice geoms' `priority` (without it MuJoCo takes the robot's own friction and the ice does nothing: `test_friction.py`, and `test_navenv.py` checks the loaded training model). The batched env applies the same deterministic push schedule as `mcrobot.pushes` (trigger x, direction, force, duration from `arena_route.json`; `test_navenv.py` checks the trigger, the force, the duration, that a push fires once per episode and that a start beyond a trigger skips it). Evaluation reports failures per course section, so a policy that only succeeds because it never meets the push zone shows up there.
Limits: the pushes are fixed (same size, place and timing every lap), so the policy can learn to expect them; they are a robustness check, not random disturbances. Stairs are still a ramp until M2. The earlier v1 arena results (if any) are v1 and are labelled as such.

## C9. "Constraints were silently dropped, so the physics was wrong" (MuJoCo Warp buffer overflow)

What went wrong once: the first v2 training chunk (light lane) printed "nefc overflow - please increase njmax" over a thousand times. MuJoCo Warp had per-world constraint and contact buffers that were too small for the G1 pressed against block geometry, and when a buffer is full the solver drops constraints and carries on, so the world keeps simulating with contacts missing. That chunk was moved to `results/_trash/nav_v2_overflow/` and not used. Controls now in place: `NJMAX` / `NCONMAX` in `navenv.py` are sized from measured peaks on arena v2 (`scripts/probe_nav_overflow.py`: a scripted lap through every section plus a random-command stress phase) with headroom; `NavEnv.step` checks the solver's own per-world overflow flag after every nav step and raises `PhysicsOverflow` instead of warning; `test_nav_overflow.py` checks that a short walk through every section of arena v2 overflows nothing and that a too-small buffer raises.
Affected earlier results: none were cited. The v1 run never trained (its log is only a queue message). The 2-iteration CPU smoke run on the v1 arena (8 envs) did not save its output, so whether it overflowed is unknown: [rerun pending] if it is ever cited; no result from it is used.
Limit: the check proves no buffer overflowed in the runs that are checked, not that the buffers suffice for every possible pose; a fall into a corner could in principle exceed the measured peaks, which is why an overflow stops the run rather than being counted.

## C10. "It was just retrained." (M2: is it still the open base policy, or a new policy that happens to climb?)

The objection has two halves: (a) the stair policy is a different policy from scratch and "fine-tuned" is a label, so the M1 statement "frozen open walking policy" no longer describes the nav stack; (b) the gain comes from more training compute, not from anything about the base or the curriculum.
Answer (planned): the fine-tuned policy starts as the base (an identity test) and stays measurably close to it, and a from-scratch run with the same recipe and budget is the control.
Tests (`docs/m2-plan.md` sections 3, 8.3, 11):
1. Identity at iteration 0: the imported actor equals the base ONNX within 1e-5 on 10 000 recorded observations (`test_m2_import.py`).
2. Provenance: the run log records, at every evaluation, the relative weight distance from the base per layer and the behavioural distance (action difference on recorded flat observations: mean and 95th percentile). Only latest, best and final checkpoints are kept (NUC rule), so the trajectory lives in the log.
3. Control: the same recipe (curriculum, rewards, budget) from a randomly initialised actor. Reported next to the fine-tuned run: stairs success, the regression gate and env-steps to reach each level. If the scratch run matches both, the claim shrinks to "the base is a convenient start"; if it does not, the base initialisation is what mattered. Either way the table is published, not one row of it.
4. Wording after M2: the nav stack uses the fine-tuned policy as its frozen low level; M1 results used the base and are labelled as such.
Limit: one training seed unless the budget allows more; the CIs then cover evaluation noise, not run-to-run variance.

## C11. "Stairs are a trivial terrain curriculum."

The objection: pyramid stairs are a standard terrain in every locomotion framework, so climbing them is not a result; or the policy only learned the generator's stairs and would fail on anything else.
Answer (planned): the claim is narrower and measured: a riser of 0.25 m, up and down, on stairs built from exported Minecraft boxes the generator never produced as a whole, with the failure frontier shown.
Tests (`docs/m2-plan.md` section 8.2):
1. Held-out geometry: treads 0.35, 0.65 and 0.8 m (the training treads are 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0 plus jitter), a riser jitter of 0.02 m, a flight with mixed risers; success with Wilson intervals.
2. Transfer to the real arena: the scripted lap and the nav evaluation run on the exporter's merged boxes (not the training generator's boxes); the merged-vs-per-block comparison bounds seam effects.
3. Difficulty is shown, not asserted: success against riser height (0.0625 to 0.30 m) and tread (0.25, 0.5, 1.0 m), up and down, the frontier included; plus a baseline fine-tune on the framework's own stairs preset (risers up to 0.10 m) evaluated at 0.25 m. The baseline is expected to fail at 0.25 m; that is a hypothesis until it is run.
4. What "blind" means is stated: plan A uses no terrain input; if plan B (height scan) is needed, the result is reported as a perception-based policy with its costs, not as the same blind policy.

## C12. "It forgot how to walk on flat ground."

The objection: fine-tuning on a hard task degrades what the base did well (catastrophic forgetting), so the arena's other sections silently get worse.
Answer (planned): a regression gate with margins fixed before training, on paired seeds, covering the original task, every other arena section and the limit probes; plus a control that shows the gate can fail.
Tests (`docs/m2-plan.md` sections 6, 8.3):
1. R1, the original flat velocity task at the deployed command ranges: survival and tracking error against the base on identical seeds; non-inferiority margins 2 points (survival) and 10 % (tracking error).
2. R2, every non-stairs section of arena v2 plus the whole lap with the ramp, scripted pursuit with perturbations: paired success difference within 3 points, lateral deviation within 1.25x.
3. R3, the envelope: ice friction at failure, push force at failure, 1-block beam, micro-steps: not worse than one grid step.
4. Positive control for the test itself: a short fine-tune on stairs only with no anchor and no flat tiles must FAIL R1 or R2. A gate that cannot fail proves nothing.
5. Checkpoint selection uses the gate: `best` is the best stairs score among checkpoints that pass it.
Limit: the flat tests are in simulation with the randomisation of the original task; they bound forgetting in that distribution, not on a robot.

## C13. "The navigator never saw the stairs as walkable." (found while planning M2)

What would go wrong: the navigation map marks a cell as a wall when its top is more than 0.25 m above the global floor (`navtask.WALL_ABOVE`), so with real 0.25 m steps the second step and the 1.5 m platform are walls: the compass route stops at the stairs and the wall rays show them as obstacles, however well the low level climbs.
Planned control: walkability from the step between neighbouring cells (`docs/m2-plan.md` section 10.3), tested by: the START-to-FINISH route of arena v2 with real stairs is connected; the SLALOM pillars and DOORWAY walls stay walls; the ramp mode produces a byte-identical map and route to today's; the per-section failure statistics of the nav evaluation show STAIRS as its own row.

## Honest limits (stated up front)

- Everything is simulation. A policy that works here may not work on a robot; M4 measures that, and until then no real-robot claim is made.
- Minecraft is proprietary (Mojang EULA). The project ships no Minecraft code or assets; users supply their own copy.
- Blocks are coarse. Terrain finer than a block (or a tenth of a block with the scale option) is out of scope.
